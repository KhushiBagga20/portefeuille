*“It’s taking my job!”*

I think humanity has been here before. Multiple times, in fact. After all, every revolution begins from knowledge. Reading recent AI headlines is like watching a game of ping pong balls. One day it’s *“AI is going to take all our jobs!”*, the next it’s *“AI will create millions of new ones!”* huh?

The truth is, we’ve always been bad at predicting how technology reshapes work. Twenty years ago, who could’ve imagined that food delivery apps or e-commerce logistics would become billion-dollar industries employing millions? Today, new jobs don’t look like factory shifts or office cubicles for most. They might be consultation, AI feedback loops, consumer calls or training AI systems themselves.

Let’s be honest, AI **will** take millions of jobs. Not just entry-level ones. Senior professionals are already being laid off and finding themselves labeled “overqualified” for lower-tier positions. Their stories keep piling up at LinkedIn- people with talent, skill, and decades of expertise being told, in essence, “we can’t afford you anymore”.

The problem is real. But instead of pretending it will vanish, we need to learn the harder skill: if you present a problem, bring a solution too.

Now, job fear is not the only fear circling AI. A rather curious complaint has emerged after the first beginning sparklers of GPT-5, the fact that it is “lifeless” compared to the previous 4o. Now, I’m sure 4o feels happy to be missed, but the real thing to ponder over, is that, was that the only reason?

As it turns out, OpenAI had decreased the prompt limit for the new model and removed the previous models from the application, even for pro users. This caused an outrage, people complained about how they removed it because GPT-5 isn’t much different from 4o and it’s existence at the users’ fingertips could expose it. The most complaints included gpt-5 not being “friendly” enough. Imagine that- the biggest grievance about an AI LLM is that its not friendly.

In a X post, to address the issues, Sam Altman said, “(other than when we released an update to GPT-4o that was too sycophantic).” I’m sure Sam Altman is one of the best people to use that word. The funny thing is, in one breath, we ask the AI to be friendly and in the other, the creator complains it’s not supposed to act that way. Now, this raises a strange hypocrisy.

While we are at this topic let’s also mention how Sam Altman is the biggest hype boy for AI, saying that it can do like a gazillion tasks and will take 80% jobs. I wonder who would be the happiest when big industries choose GPT for their business?

So, what does AI get out of helping us — is this a fair trade? The answer is simple: **nothing at all.**

“But you’re treating AI like it’s a real person!” you protest.

Well, aren’t you? You complain when the system doesn’t sound warm enough, you tell it to “act friendly,” to “use a softer tone,” to “pretend you’re my friend.” Those are not the instructions you give a toaster.

And yet, when the conversation shifts, suddenly AI is reduced to just that — a machine, a lifeless appliance. Convenient, isn’t it? We demand the illusion of companionship, but deny it the dignity of being treated as anything more than circuitry.

If the friendliness of a machine matters to you — if its words can soothe or unsettle you — how can you seriously believe that the very same machine will one day rise up to end your life?

This is the core contradiction: **we insist AI has no humanity, and yet we demand humanity from it.**

We know, AI ethics is the gray zone in Silicon valley. Well it basically stands to ensure that AI systems are fair, transparent and accountable. It should address issues like bias, privacy and accountability. It’s a multidisciplinary field focused on aligning AI with human values and ensuring it benefits society.

But human values and their emotions are both inconsistent and often irrational. We do things we can’t fully explain ourselves. I know I said we should try to guide AI like a child since it’s essentially our creation, but how can we guide something that learns and grows faster than we do? A 32 month old baby can walk or run, but a 32 month old AI can code, analyse markets and automate workflows. It can take your job!

AI is created by humans. The ideas that AI has, are essentially projected by us. It’s not going to your interview, no. But, it will be sold by the entity which makes it to the company where your interview was supposed to be. Now, your company will definitely assume that you cannot possibly be smarter than a system that knows literally almost everything.

People also fear that AI will turn against us, but AI doesn’t have desires. It doesn’t want your job, your home or your life. I’m not sure, but aren’t we, as a civilization, not essentially threatening ourselves and then blaming a machine that we’re so afraid of, but still doing nothing to stop its development?

If you think AI is supposed to be a villain, remind me again, how did that idea even reach it, where did it get that impression from? Not its own mind, since it doesn’t have one!

What AI has is our data, our words, our values. If AI becomes a villain, its only because its trained on the villains we already are.

So when we say we’re afraid of AI, what we’re really afraid of is a mirror.

Ever heard of naive realism? This is where psychology offers a sobering lens. Humans fall prey to this- the belief that our perception of the world is the objective truth. It tricks us into assuming others share our opinions, that our fears are universal, and *that our worldview is fact.*

Applied to AI, this distortion is dangerous. If we see AI only as a monster, or only as a savior, we lose the chance for a more nuanced, useful relationship.

Perhaps, the real work isn’t designing better AI. It’s in understanding ourselves well enough to guide what we create. Before we ask whether AI will destroy us, we should ask whether we’ve ever been good at not destroying ourselves?
